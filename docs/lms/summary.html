Itoju Clinics is a fictional organisation and every record in the portfolio is synthetic.

1. Selected organisation

OrganisationItoju Clinics (ìtọ́jú is Yoruba for care): a simulated network of twelve outpatient clinics and diagnostic laboratories in Lagos, Abuja, Ibadan, Enugu and Kano, each with its own pharmacy and laboratory.
Business and systemThe database is the network's clinical and operational system of record: patients, appointments against clinicians' time slots, consultations, prescribing and dispensing, laboratory orders and results, stock, billing, and an audit trail of who opened which record.
UsersPatients (through the booking app or front desk), receptionists, clinicians, pharmacists, laboratory scientists, billing officers, medical-records and data-protection officers, analysts (de-identified data only), internal auditors, the clinic application's service account and the database administrator.
Major dataPatients with phones, allergies, next of kin and consents; facilities, departments, clinicians and slots; appointments and their history; encounters with vital signs, diagnoses and append-only notes; drugs, prescriptions and a stock ledger; lab tests, orders and results; invoices and payments in kobo; a record-access log.
Key operationsRegister a patient; book without ever double-booking a clinician; record an encounter; prescribe and dispense without negative stock; order and result a lab test; transfer stock; invoice and take payment; show a patient's timeline; produce the daily schedule and the diagnosis, reorder and turnaround reports.

3. Evidence

4. Individual technical reflection

An outpatient network gave every topic real work: integrity for clinical records, concurrency for clinicians' time, a ledger for stock and in-country distribution for health data. I would keep the named rule behind every constraint, the stock ledger with its guarded cache, and deterministic data with result hashes, which made every before-and-after comparison like for like. The hardest problems were found by checks rather than by reading code: data that differed between loads because sequences are never rolled back, consistency checks that were fine on sample data and quadratic at volume, and a promoted standby that waited for itself because it had inherited the primary's settings.

  1. A test is only as good as the volume it runs at.
  2. Buffers are a more stable measure than milliseconds, and an index helps only a selective query.
  3. Deadlocks come from lock order: the same 400 transfers caused 100 deadlocks in an arbitrary order and none in a fixed one.
  4. Measure an outage from the client's acknowledged writes, and test failover end to end.

Next, I would add automatic failover with Patroni, real network delay between the clusters, and continuous change data capture from PostgreSQL to the MongoDB read model.

5. AI use declaration

I used Claude Code (Anthropic), running the Claude Opus 5.5 model, as a coding and writing assistant. I chose the case study and specified the architecture, technology stack, data conventions and order of work; I made the design decisions, approved a plan before each part and committed every change myself. The assistant generated the scripts and drafted the documents to my specification. Every output was run and captured unedited, and every figure in the portfolio comes from an evidence file. 339 automated checks and comparisons pass, MongoDB was cross-checked against PostgreSQL, and the real errors those checks found were corrected. The full declaration and a log of each step are in the repository.
