# Is a document database right for Itoju Clinics?

For some of its data, yes. For the data that has to be right to the last tablet, no. I judged each kind of data on the factors the course uses for choosing a database (its data model, the consistency it needs, how it is queried, and how it must scale), and I tested the answer by loading the same 714,247 encounters into both systems and asking both the same questions. Every MongoDB answer matched PostgreSQL's row for row.

**Where the document model fits.** A visit is naturally a document: the vital signs, diagnoses, notes and prescriptions are written in one consultation and read together. MongoDB returned a whole visit in one read of one document (2.7 ms, [p4-04](outputs/p4-04-workload-after.txt)), where the relational schema joins five tables. With an index on `(patient_id, started_at)`, a clinician's timeline examined exactly 20 documents. Laboratory results change shape from test to test, and a single collection held both numeric and qualitative results, each checked against its own rules by a validator. A multikey index on `diagnoses.code` brought the asthma-by-branch query from 1,929 ms to 17 ms. Home blood-pressure readings suited a time-series collection: 25,200 readings were stored in 1,400 buckets ([p4-05](outputs/p4-05-timeseries.txt)). The same 779 MB of documents took 190 MB on disk once compressed.

**Where it does not.** The rules that protect patients and money cross documents, and MongoDB validates one document at a time. Nothing in MongoDB stops an encounter naming a patient who does not exist, or two documents booking the same clinician for the same half-hour. In PostgreSQL, foreign keys and the exclusion constraint make both impossible, whatever the application does. Multi-document transactions close part of the gap: dispensing and invoicing committed or aborted together, while the same failure without a transaction left an invoice for 2,396 tablets that were never dispensed ([p4-07](outputs/p4-07-transactions.txt)). But MongoDB does not queue conflicting writers. The second writer to touch the same stock document failed with `WriteConflict` after 3 ms and had to be retried (11 attempts by `with_transaction`), where PostgreSQL made it wait. Time-series collections cannot be written inside a transaction at all. Large aggregations were slower too. The monthly diagnosis report took 3.4 seconds in MongoDB even with an index, because the date range covers half the collection, and MongoDB's planner chose the index although it examined 349,504 documents. In Portfolio 2, PostgreSQL's cost-based optimiser declined an index that would have matched two in three encounters, and answered the same report in about a second, or 7 ms from a materialised view.

**ACID, DBASE and CAP.** With `writeConcern: majority` and snapshot reads, MongoDB transactions are ACID, and a replica set then behaves as a CP system: during a partition, a primary that cannot reach a majority steps down rather than accept writes that might be lost. Relaxing that (writing with `w: 1`, reading from secondaries) buys availability and latency at the cost of the course's DBASE properties: basically available, soft state, eventually consistent (BASE in most of the literature). That trade-off suits analytics and patient-facing reads such as appointment lists. It does not suit stock, bookings or payments, where a stale read leads straight to a double booking or a negative shelf.

**Other NoSQL models.** Two more stores would earn a place at network scale. A key-value store such as Redis fits short-lived data: login sessions, and the five-minute hold placed on a slot while a patient completes an online booking. Both expire on their own and need no joins. A wide-column store such as Cassandra fits national-scale monitoring: if every hypertensive patient sent readings, writes would arrive by the million each day, always appended, always read by patient and time. A partition key of patient and day gives exactly that, with tunable consistency. Referral pathways are a graph. `$graphLookup` followed them correctly here: it matched an independent breadth-first search on all 1,175 edges ([p4-06](outputs/p4-06-graph.txt)). But it must fit in 100 MB of memory, so deep pathway analysis at scale belongs in a graph database with index-free adjacency.

**Conclusion: polyglot persistence.** PostgreSQL stays the system of record for appointments, encounters, stock, billing and the audit trail, because its constraints and transactions are what keep that data correct. MongoDB earns its place as a read model: the patient timeline and visit documents for the clinic and booking apps, laboratory results of any shape, and the home-monitoring time series. A key-value store holds sessions and booking holds. The honest cost is keeping copies in step. The full copy here took 5.4 minutes, and reconciling it meant counting and summing across the whole of both stores. In production, PostgreSQL's logical replication would feed MongoDB continuously and one way, so the document model would always be slightly behind the database it is copied from: eventual consistency, by design.
